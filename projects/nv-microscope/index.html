A 3D model of the lab and the instrument, from the shielded room to the probe tip

The room

A quiet box for one spin.

The whole setup lives inside one room-sized Faraday cage. Electronic noise from outside stops at the walls, and the temperature holds within ±1 °C.

Against vibration

The instruments hang.

The computer, the microwave generator and the amplifier are all suspended from the ceiling, right above the optics, to avoid vibration.

Still air

Curtain, then cage.

The two optical tables sit behind a black curtain inside a second cage, to avoid airflow around the beam.

Automation

Load the sample. Walk away.

Control software I wrote in Python, LabVIEW and MATLAB runs the laser pulses, the microwave chain, the optical scan and the nano-positioning. Once the sample is loaded, a measurement runs start to finish on its own.

Table one

Light, in pulses.

A green laser passes through an acousto-optic modulator, which switches it on and off in short pulses. A single optical fibre carries the light to the second table.

Table two

A confocal microscope, and the AFM.

A galvo mirror scans the focus across the sample. Green light makes the NV centre in the probe glow red; that glow comes back to a single-photon detector, which fires a pulse for every photon it catches. Counting the pulses gives the brightness. The AFM and its nano-positioning stage share this table.

Down to the sample

Follow the beam.

At the far end of the same table, a periscope drops the beam into the objective of the AFM. Underneath, a stick-slip stage brings the sample into place and a closed-loop piezo scanner rasters it, nanometre by nanometre.

The probe

A diamond on a tuning fork.

The NV probe hangs from the top on a quartz tuning fork, which holds the diamond tip nanometres above the sample: a thin magnetic film of YIG under a gold stripline. A waveguide probe lands on the stripline, and its microwaves launch spin waves through the film.

At the tip

One spin, reading the waves.

A single NV centre at the apex, above the YIG film. Each spin wave's field rotates, and the hand depends on its direction: σ+ heading left, σ− heading right. With the microwave on the σ+ line, the NV dims over the left-going wave and stays bright over the right-going one.

The lab: the setup sits in a room-sized Faraday cage held within ±1 °C; the computer, microwave generator and amplifier are suspended from the ceiling above the optics; the two optical tables are enclosed by a black curtain and a second cage against airflow; and control software (Python, LabVIEW, MATLAB) runs the whole measurement automatically once the sample is loaded. The instrument: a 532 nm laser, half-wave plate, beam splitter and acousto-optic modulator, coupled by fibre to the second table: a dichroic mirror, galvo scanner and 4f relay, then, on the same table, a periscope down into a 50x objective. Under the objective, the AFM: the NV probe on a tuning fork above the sample, which sits on stick-slip coarse stages and a piezo XYZ scanner, with a waveguide probe on the stripline. Red light returns through the dichroic to a single-photon counter.
